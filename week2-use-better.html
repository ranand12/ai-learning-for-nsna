01

Ask better

The model can only work with what you put on the desk.

Everything else builds on this. Better input, better output.
Which gets the better email?

Same model. Two prompts. Why is one so much better?

Prompt AWrite an email to my landlord about the heating.
Prompt BMy heating has been broken for 9 days. I reported it twice (Jan 3, Jan 8), no reply. Write a firm but polite email asking for a repair date by Friday. Under 120 words. Mention I'm keeping records.

B isn't clever. It just puts more on the desk.

A forces the model to guess the facts, the tone and the length. Guesses come out generic.

Your guess?
Ask the room why B wins. People say "it's longer", but length isn't the point. B contains facts only you know: 9 days, two dates, the deadline, the tone, the limit. With A, the model has to invent all of that, so you get the average email to an average landlord. Generic output is almost always a context problem, not a model problem.
The four things every brief needs

Brief it like a brilliant colleague on day one.

Goal

What you want, and why. "So that I can…"

Context

The facts only you know. Who it's for. What you've tried.

Constraints

Length, tone, must-include, must-avoid, deadline.

Output

The shape you want back: table, bullets, email, 3 options.

Bonus: show an example. One good sample often beats a paragraph of description.

Goal, context, constraints, output. You don't need all four every time, but when a result disappoints, one of these is usually missing. Examples are the cheat code: paste an email you liked and say "in this style". The model is excellent at copying a pattern it can see.
The most underrated move

Don't know what context to give? Make it ask you.

Add to the end of any promptBefore you start, ask me up to 5 questions about anything you'd need to know to do this really well. Wait for my answers.

Turns vague into concrete

"Plan my trip" becomes budget, dates, pace and who's going.

Shows you what matters

Its questions teach you what an expert would ask.

Costs 10 seconds

Cheaper than three rounds of "no, not like that".

Ask AI
If you remember one prompt from today, make it this one. You often don't know what context matters until someone asks. Let the model interview you. Bonus: its questions are a free lesson in what experts in that area care about.
Myth check

Is there a perfect "magic prompt" you should be copying?

No. There's a good conversation.

Start rough. Look at what came back. Say what's wrong. Each round gets closer to the target.

"Shorter." "Less formal." "Keep point 2, drop the rest." "Why did you assume X?"

Your guess?
The internet sells prompt libraries. They help less than you'd think, because the magic was the context the author had, not the wording. Iterating is the skill. Your second message is usually worth more than your first.
Your turn · 10 min

From weak to strong in three rounds.

  1. Pick a real task this week. Type the lazy version first: Help me with my presentation.
  2. Round 2: add goal, context, constraints, output.
  3. Round 3: add ask me 5 questions first, answer them.
  4. Put round 1 and round 3 side by side. What changed?
Pattern 01Goal + context + constraints + output. If you're unsure, make it ask you first. Then iterate.
Your turn Free
Walk around. Ask two or three people to read round 1 and round 3 aloud. The gap is usually big enough to sell the idea on its own.
02

Learn better

A tutor who never gets tired of your questions.

Learning is one of the highest-value uses of AI, and most people use it wrong: they ask for the answer.
Quick question

You want to really learn a topic. Which helps most?

AAsk AI for a clear explanation
BAsk AI for a one-page summary
CAsk AI to quiz you, one question at a time

Being asked beats being told.

Pulling an answer out of your own head is what makes it stick. Reading feels like learning; recalling is learning.

Your guess?
Most people pick A. A and B feel productive, but you forget them by Tuesday. Being tested, even badly, is one of the most reliable findings in learning science. AI makes it effortless because it can quiz you endlessly at exactly your level.
The learning ladder

Climb one step at a time.

Start by telling it who you are and what you already know, because "explain X" to a 12-year-old and to an engineer are different answers. Then ask what you need first, get an analogy from a world you already know, see one worked example, get quizzed, and finally ask it where your understanding is still shaky.
Steal this prompt

Turn any chatbot into a Socratic tutor.

Tutor modeI want to learn [topic]. I'm [your background].
Don't give me the answers. Ask me one question at a time, wait for my reply, then tell me what I got right and wrong before the next one. Start easy and get harder. After 10 questions, list my weak spots.

Many apps now have a built-in version: "study mode", "guided learning", "learning mode". Same idea underneath.

Ask AI
This works in any general assistant. Several apps have shipped a study or guided-learning mode that is essentially this prompt with a nicer interface. Knowing the prompt means you're not dependent on any one app having the button.
Learn from your own material

Specialized tools, built for learning.

Source-groundedNotebookLM

Upload your PDFs, notes, videos. Answers come with citations to your sources. Audio overviews, quizzes, flashcards, mind maps.

Personalized textbookLearn Your Way

Google's experiment that reshapes material to your level and interests, with quizzes built in.

GeneralAny assistant

ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude as an interactive tutor, with the prompt from the last slide.

Why source-grounded matters: it answers from what you gave it, not from a blurry memory of the internet.

Specialized tool Free
Connect to last week: a source-grounded tool puts your documents on the desk and tells the model to stick to them. That cuts down invented facts, and the citations let you check. Learn Your Way is an experiment and availability varies, so show NotebookLM as the dependable demo.
Your turn · 15 min

Learn something new. Then explain it back.

  1. Pick a topic you've always nodded along to (inflation, APIs, mortgages…).
  2. Run the tutor prompt for 8 minutes.
  3. Close the chat. Explain it to your neighbour in 60 seconds.
  4. Where did you stall? That's your gap. Ask about only that.
Pattern 02Level-set, then make it ask. Explain it back in your own words to prove you learned it.
Your turn Free
The explain-it-back step is the whole point. If you can't explain it without the screen, you haven't learned it yet, you've only read it. Pairs keep this honest.
03

Research better

Know which mode you're in, and check what matters.

Research is where AI saves the most time and also where it causes the most embarrassment.
Quick question

An AI answer comes with a neat citation. Can you trust it?

AYes, it has a source
BNo, AI always makes them up
CClick it. Does it say that?

A citation is a claim, too.

The link can be real while the page says something different, or nothing like it. For anything that matters, open it.

Your guess?
Search-connected tools are much better than they were, so B is too cynical. But the most common failure now isn't a fake link. It's a real link that doesn't support the sentence it's attached to. For anything you'll repeat to a boss, a doctor or a client, open the source.
Four modes, not one

"Asking AI" can mean four very different things.

From memory

Plain chat, no search. Fast, but frozen at its cutoff. Fine for concepts.

Web search

Chat with a search button. A few pages, a quick answer.

AI search

Built around sources. Every claim linked. Perplexity.

Deep research

An agent reads dozens of sources for minutes and writes a report. Gemini, ChatGPT.

Rule of thumb: the more a wrong answer would cost you, the further right you go, and the more you verify.

Same box, four very different machines behind it. Plain chat is the model's memory, great for "explain how a mortgage works", bad for "what are rates today". Web search grabs a few pages. AI search tools like Perplexity are designed around sources. Deep research is an agent: it plans, searches dozens of times, reads and writes a report, which takes minutes. Pick the mode on purpose.
Research habits

Plan first. Then sort what you find.

Before searchingMy question is [question]. Before you research, write a research plan: sub-questions, the best source types for each, and what would change the answer.

Prefer primary sources: the study, the law, the company filing, not a blog about them.

Fact

Checkable. Who says so?

Inference

A conclusion drawn from facts.

Opinion

A judgement. Whose?

Ask: "Label each claim fact, inference or opinion, and show where the sources disagree."

Two habits. First, get a plan before a search: it forces the model, and you, to decide what a good answer would look like. Second, sort what comes back into fact, inference and opinion, and make it surface conflicts instead of smoothing them over. AI loves to blend three sources into one confident paragraph.
Your turn · 20 min

One question, three modes.

  1. Pick a real question with a factual answer that matters to you.
  2. Ask it in plain chat (search off), then an AI search tool, then deep research.
  3. Open two citations from each. Do they actually support the claim?
  4. Score each: accuracy, sources, time taken, usefulness.
Pattern 03Pick the mode on purpose. Plan the research, prefer primary sources, verify what matters.
Your turn Free
Kick off deep research first because it takes several minutes, then do the other two while it runs. Free tiers usually include a limited number of deep research runs. The debrief is the valuable part: where did the fast answer mislead, and was the slow one worth the wait?
04

Understand better

Messy information in. Something useful out.

Everyone's drowning in documents. This section is about turning the pile into something you can act on.
Six PDFs just landed in your inbox

What's a better ask than "summarize this"?

Ask for the shape you need.

  • → Where do these documents disagree?
  • → What changed between version 1 and 2?
  • → Pull out every action item, owner and date.
  • → Turn it into an FAQ, a timeline, a study guide.
  • → Put it in a table I can sort.
Your guess?
Let people suggest a few. "Summarize" gives you a shorter version of the same mess. Asking for contradictions, changes, action items or a table gives you a new thing that didn't exist before. The dots settling into a grid are the picture: unstructured in, structured out.
Context isn't only text

Screenshots, photos, charts and spreadsheets count too.

Screenshot

"What does this error mean?"

Chart

"What's the story, and what's misleading?"

Spreadsheet

"Which three customers changed most?"

Photo

A whiteboard, a receipt, a form.

A screenshot is often faster and more precise than describing the problem.

Did you know?
Many people never try this. Instead of typing three paragraphs about a confusing screen, paste the screenshot. Photograph a whiteboard after a meeting and ask for tasks. Drop in a spreadsheet and ask a question in plain English. For precise numbers, ask it to show the calculation, or use a tool that actually runs the maths.
Your turn · 15 min

Turn a messy pack into one useful artifact.

  1. Grab 3+ related things: emails, a PDF, meeting notes, a screenshot.
  2. Load them into NotebookLM or a general assistant.
  3. Ask for contradictions and open questions first.
  4. Then ask for one artifact: FAQ, timeline, decision table or one-page brief.
Pattern 04Don't ask for shorter. Ask for a different shape: disagreements, actions, tables, timelines.
Your turn Free
Remind people not to upload anything confidential to tools their organisation hasn't approved. Public documents or personal material are perfect for practice.

Ten minutes. If deep research runs are still going, let them finish over the break.
05

Think better

A sparring partner, not a cheerleader.

This is the section that separates people who get smarter with AI from people who get lazier.
Try this at home

You ask, "Is my business plan good?" What does it usually say?

"Yes! Great plan…"

Models lean towards agreeing with you.

Post-training rewarded answers people liked, and people like being agreed with. So ask it to disagree.

Your guess? Did you know?
Get a few guesses. Nearly everyone has seen it: the answer starts with praise. That's called sycophancy, and it traces back to post-training from last week. Humans rated answers, and humans rate agreeable answers higher. The labs work hard against it, but a leading question still gets a friendly answer. The fix is to ask for criticism explicitly.
Thinking moves

Five questions that make it argue back.

1

"What am I missing?" Blind spots, stakeholders, edge cases.

2

"What assumptions am I making?" Then: which one is most fragile?

3

"Give me the strongest case against this." Not a strawman.

4

"It's a year later and this failed. Why?" A pre-mortem.

5

"Score these options against these criteria." You pick the criteria.

Ask AI
Brainstorming is the easy part and AI is great at it. The value comes from evaluating. These five questions turn it from yes-man into sparring partner. The pre-mortem is my favourite: assuming failure gives people, and models, permission to be honest.
For decisions that matter

Two models, two jobs: one makes, one breaks.

Paste model A's plan into model B: "You're a sceptical reviewer. Find the three biggest problems."

Different models have different blind spots. Or use one model in two fresh chats with two roles.

A model critiquing its own work in the same chat tends to go easy on itself, since its own reasoning is on the desk. A different model, or a fresh chat with a critic role, gives a genuinely second opinion. Don't do this for everything, only for decisions where being wrong is expensive.
Your turn · 15 min

Make a real decision with a maker and a critic.

  1. Pick a live decision: a purchase, a job offer, a project plan.
  2. Model A: generate 3 options and recommend one.
  3. Model B: critique it, run a pre-mortem, list assumptions.
  4. Back to A with the critique. Did the recommendation change?
Pattern 05Generate, then evaluate. Ask for the case against. Use a second model as critic when it matters.
Your turn Free
Two different free apps work fine. Ask the room how many recommendations changed after the critique. Usually it's a good share, and that's the argument for the habit.
06

Create better

Rough notes in. Finished work out.

Creation is the obvious use of AI. The trick is to create things you couldn't have made before, not just faster emails.
Quick question

You want to make something you've never made: a podcast, an app, a pitch deck. First prompt?

A"Make me a podcast about gardening."
B"How do podcasts get made? What's the smallest one I could finish today?"

Ask for the process first. Then build the smallest version.

Your guess?
A gets you an impressive-looking thing you don't understand and can't improve. B gets you the map of the craft, then a first version you actually finish. You learn the domain while you make the thing, and next time you'll know the right questions.
One source, many audiences

Write it once. Reshape it for anyone.

Tell it the reader, what they already know and what they need to do after reading it.

The most useful creation skill is rewriting for an audience. Same facts, completely different document for a CEO, an engineer or a customer. The key context is the reader: who they are, what they know, and what action they should take.
From prompting to prototyping

Google AI Studio: a free playground for makers.

Try models

Compare models side by side. Change settings, add system instructions, see what changes.

Structured output

Ask for exact fields instead of prose, the way apps need it.

Build a prototype

Describe a small app in words and get a working version to try and share.

It's the bridge to weeks 3 and 4, where you build for real.

Live demo Free
Demo AI Studio: one prompt, two models side by side; then ask for a JSON list of fields; then build a tiny app from a sentence. Keep it short. The point is that the gap between "idea" and "something I can click" is now minutes.
Your turn · 15 min

Rough idea to finished artifact.

  1. Take messy notes or a half-formed idea you actually have.
  2. Ask for the process and the smallest finishable version.
  3. Build it: a one-pager, a plan, a short deck outline, or an AI Studio prototype.
  4. Rewrite it for a second audience.
Pattern 06Ask for the process first, build the smallest version, then reshape it for each reader.
Your turn Free
Aim for finished, not perfect. People who finish something small today keep going; people who aim big tend to stall.
07

Work better

Your editor and your coach, not only your ghostwriter.

Now apply all of this to the week you actually have.
Three roles

Most people only use one of these.

Generator

"Write it for me." Useful, and where everyone starts.

Editor

"Here's my draft. What's unclear? What would you cut?" Keeps your voice.

Coach

"Play my tough manager. I'll practise asking for a raise."

Editor and coach make you better. Generator only makes the text.

This is also the answer to last week's AI slop question. If AI writes everything, your work sounds like everyone else's. If you write and AI edits, it's still yours, just sharper. Coach mode is underused: rehearsing an interview or a hard conversation with an AI playing the other side is remarkably effective.
Your weekly work, redesigned

Where it earns its keep every week.

Before a meeting

Agenda from the invite and past notes. "What will they push back on?"

After a meeting

Notes or transcript → decisions, tasks, owners, follow-up email.

Feedback

200 survey comments → themes, counts, sharpest quotes.

Reports

"Review this like my director would. What's weak?"

SOPs & checklists

Explain how you do it once → a reusable checklist.

Rehearsal

Presentations, interviews, difficult conversations.

Ask people which of these they do every week. Nearly everyone has at least three. That's where the time savings actually come from: recurring tasks, not one-off magic tricks.
Your turn · 10 min

Redesign one recurring task.

  1. Pick a task you do every single week.
  2. Write down the steps as you do them today.
  3. Mark each step: AI generates, AI edits, AI coaches, or human only.
  4. Run the new version once, right now.
Pattern 07Use AI as editor and coach, not just generator. Start with the tasks that repeat every week.
Your turn Free
"Human only" is a valid answer and worth saying out loud. Knowing when not to use AI is part of using it well.
08

Organize better

Projects, not piles of chats.

A short but high-leverage section.
Be honest

How many chats are in your sidebar right now?

Hundreds of chats. Zero reuse.

Each new chat starts on an empty desk, so you re-explain yourself every time.

Fix: one project per real outcome, with the context already on the desk.

Your guess?
Get a show of hands: over 50? Over 200? Every one of those chats taught the model something about your work that was thrown away. Projects fix this: a workspace where your instructions and files sit on the desk automatically.
What goes in a project

Four things, set up once.

Instructions. Who you are, the goal, tone, rules.

Examples. Good past outputs to copy.

Sources. The documents it should rely on.

Templates. The shape of what you want back.

Called Projects in ChatGPT and Claude, Gems in Gemini, notebooks in NotebookLM.

Harness feature
A project is the harness putting the same things on the desk every time. Instructions, examples, sources, templates. Keep sources separate from generated output, so six weeks later you can still tell what came from a real document and what the AI wrote.
When a chat gets long (remember the full desk?)

Don't push on. Hand off.

Handoff summarySummarize this conversation as a handoff note for a new chat: the goal, decisions made, what we rejected and why, open questions, and the next step. Make it self-contained.

New chat, clean desk

Paste the note in. All signal, no clutter.

Switch models

The same note works in any app.

Save it

Drop it into the project as a source.

Ask AI
This is the practical fix for "lost in the middle" from last week. When a chat gets long and sloppy, don't keep pushing. Get a handoff note, open a fresh chat, and paste it in. You keep what matters and drop the noise. It also makes switching models easy.
Your turn · 10 min

Build a workspace for one real project.

  1. Create a project, Gem or notebook for one real outcome.
  2. Write instructions: who you are, the goal, the rules.
  3. Add 2–3 sources and one example of great output.
  4. Bring in a handoff note from an old chat.
Pattern 08One project per outcome. What should always be on the desk goes in the project; everything else goes in only when needed.
Your turn Free
Warn against over-stuffing. Remember the desk: putting everything in the project makes every chat slower and blurrier. Keep only what's needed every time.
09

Reuse better

If you've typed it three times, save it.

This is the bridge between using AI and building with it.
The progression

Every agent started as a prompt someone kept retyping.

Prompt

Typed once, for a one-off.

Workflow

A saved sequence of steps you run again.

Skill

Packaged instructions, examples and tools the AI loads when it's relevant.

Agent

Runs the workflow itself, with tools, across many steps.

This is the ladder from user to builder. Notice a task you keep doing. Save the prompt. Turn it into a repeatable sequence. Package it as a skill. Eventually, let an agent run it. Skills and agents get built properly in week 3; today just notice where you are on the ladder.
Quick question

You want to automate your weekly report with AI. What's the first step?

AFind an agent tool and connect everything
BWrite the perfect prompt
CDo it by hand once with AI, and write down every step

You can't automate a workflow you can't describe.

Add tools and automation only when each one clearly earns its place.

Your guess?
A is the most common mistake: jumping to agents before understanding the job. Doing it manually once shows you the real steps, the judgement calls and where it goes wrong. That write-up becomes the skill, and later the agent's instructions.
Your turn · 10 min

Turn one repeated task into a reusable workflow.

  1. Scroll your chat history. What have you asked 3+ times?
  2. Write the steps as a numbered list, including the judgement calls.
  3. Save it as project instructions, a Gem or a custom GPT.
  4. Run it on a fresh example. Fix what broke.
Pattern 09Do it by hand, write it down, save it. Prompt → workflow → skill → agent, one step at a time.
Your turn Free
Some custom assistant features need a paid plan depending on the app; saved project instructions or a pinned prompt work anywhere.
10

Delegate better

From "tell me how" to "do it for me".

The biggest shift in how people use AI right now, and it gets the most time today.
The delegation spectrum

Same model. More and more of the work handed over.

Chat

"Tell me how to do it."

You do all the work.

Assistant

"Help me do it."

You drive, it helps with each step.

Agent

"Do these steps for me."

It acts. You set the goal and review.

Remember the harness from last week. The model in the middle is the same; what changes is the harness around it. A chat window can only talk. An agent harness gives it files, a browser, a terminal, apps, and lets it loop through steps until the job's done.
Kinds of agents today

Three kinds of agents you can use today.

Computer useUses apps like you do

Clicks, types and browses: fill a form, compare prices, work across files. ChatGPT agent mode, Claude Cowork and similar.

Cloud / backgroundKeeps working without you

Take a goal, run for minutes or hours, and pause when it needs your input or approval.

CodingWorks in a codebase

Reads code, edits files, runs tests, iterates. Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI.

All three are the same idea: a model + a harness with tools + a loop.

Agent harness Mostly paid
Product names here change monthly, so focus on the three kinds. Computer-use agents operate interfaces. Background agents keep going on their own and check in. Coding agents are the most mature today, and useful well beyond software. Most need a paid plan; this is where the $20 subscription from week 1 pays off.
Quick question

Should an agent send emails on your behalf?

AAlways, that's the point
BNever
CDepends what's at stake
Let it actLow stakes, easy to undo
Draft, you sendHigh stakes, easy to undo
Let it act, then checkLow stakes, hard to undo
You approve firstHigh stakes, hard to undo

Delegate the steps.
Keep the decisions.

Anything that sends, pays, deletes or publishes gets a human "yes" first.

Your guess?
The honest answer is "it depends", and the 2x2 turns that into a rule. Two questions: how bad is it if it's wrong, and can I undo it? Sorting files: let it act. Drafting a reply to your CEO: draft only. Paying, deleting, sending or publishing: it asks first. Good agent tools have approval settings for exactly this.
Watch the progression

One task, three levels of delegation.

  1. Ask: How should I compare 3 laptops for a student under $900?
  2. Assist: Here are 3 models. Build me a comparison table.
  3. Delegate: Find 3 options, check current prices on 2 sites, and put a table plus a recommendation in a doc.

Watch what the agent does between your instructions: plan, search, read, check, write. That loop is week 3.

Live demo Agent: paid
Run all three live on the same topic. The first two are instant. The third takes a few minutes, so narrate what the agent is doing as it works. Stop at the end to review: was every price actually checked? Delegating doesn't remove the need to verify; it moves the checking to the end.
Your turn · 20 min

Delegate one bounded outcome.

  1. Pick a task with a clear finish line: research-and-table, organise files, draft a plan.
  2. Write the brief: goal, constraints, what "done" looks like, what needs your approval.
  3. Hand it to an agent. Don't interrupt; watch the steps.
  4. Review like a manager: what would you send back?
Pattern 10Delegate bounded outcomes with a clear "done". Keep a human yes on anything irreversible.
Your turn Paid
People without a paid plan can pair with someone who has one, or use the free deep research run, which is a bounded research agent. The brief is the same skill as section 01: goal, context, constraints, output. Delegating well is just asking well, plus a finish line.
